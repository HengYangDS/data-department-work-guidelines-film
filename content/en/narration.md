A historical price dataset is finished. But can the next person to use it rely on it? They need to know what each number means, which time it describes, what evidence it rests on, and what it may be used for. Researchers backtest with this data, and trading systems control risk with it. A number nobody can explain travels down the whole chain. Every step hands something to the next person: from acquisition and modeling, through development, production and platforms, to product management, governance and operational delivery. So the work's value lies not in a file, a table, a report or the start of a process, but in turning real-world signals into judgments, data assets and actions others can safely rely on.

The Data Department Work Guidelines are the department's contract for work quality. They target four failures. The first confuses facts with choices: data arriving late is a fact; changing vendors is a choice. The second fixes the symptom and leaves the mechanism alone: someone backfills by hand, and next month it breaks again. The third is communication that leaves no one able to decide or act. The fourth lets AI produce more material, while judgment and results grow no more reliable. So a task should achieve three things: solve the problem; test the judgment and its limits; and leave the system better able to recognize or handle the next occurrence. These are three distinct outcomes, not three required reports.

The guidelines bind members and the Agents they delegate to: AI systems that search, reason and act toward a goal. Direction, authorization, decisions and responsibility for consequences stay with people. Two kinds of authority need to stay apart: authority to act, and authority about facts. Authority to act answers who may decide what to do, and it has an order. Law, regulation, security requirements and mandatory company policy come first. Within those limits comes what the decision owner for this matter explicitly decides. Then contracts, policies, specifications and decision records in force. Plans, provisional agreements and personal preferences come last. Authority about facts answers what can establish what is true: preferably primary records and repeatable observations, with a source, time, subject and scope. Second-hand accounts, caches kept for speed, generated views built from the records, Agent output and memory are unverified, so they are only leads. Authority to act cannot make a false fact true, and a fact does not grant permission to act. If a release is set for today but verification finds the data wrong, report the conflict and its impact. Don't alter the record, and don't silently act as if one side had won. Expertise alone grants no authority to decide or act. Professional judgment belongs with people who have the competence and the facts; when either is missing, bring in the domain owner.

Four boundaries must never be crossed. First, tell the truth: no fabrication, no hiding, no cherry-picking, no uncertainty dressed as certainty. Second, stay in bounds: subject and time, competence and permission, data, security, compliance and action. Third, name the owner: every important judgment, decision, change and acceptance has an accountable person. Fourth, do not claim false completion: without current evidence matching the claim, call nothing complete, correct, usable or adopted. Inside these boundaries, autonomy, exploration and creative work are welcome. When an action would cross one, stop, make it visible, and escalate. After a breach, stop the affected action, disclose it, correct it within your authority, and escalate as the risk requires. Risk sets the process. Local, reversible work with no external commitment can close in one clear exchange that states the conclusion, basis and action. Cross-role or extended work, or work with competing options or material uncertainty, is written up in the existing work record: the problem, options, execution path and acceptance. High-risk work also needs a written decision, explicit authorization, a rollback or degradation path, independent review and human acceptance. High risk means work touching production, funds, sensitive data, security or compliance, deletion or overwrite, external commitments or irreversible consequences. Whatever the risk, every duty still applies.

Before analysis, ask which decision it must support: who decides what, and by when. Say a team wants a better data catalog. Is it that people can't find the data? Or do they find it but not know what it means, how good it is, or whether they may use it? Each needs a different fix. So frame the problem: its subject and who is affected, the present state and the baseline, the success criteria, and what is out of scope. A goal without time, cost, compliance, technical and resource limits is not yet an executable commitment. If the subject, the authority or an irreversible consequence is unclear, stop the affected action and ask an authorized person to decide. Collecting information is not the aim. Say which judgment it could change.

Six kinds of statement need to stay distinct. Take an illustrative case: last night's price collection ran two hours late. Two hours late is a recorded fact. Late publication by the vendor is an untested hypothesis. If publication was on time but the job started late, scheduling may explain the delay. That is an inference. By the timeliness standard, this delivery failed. That is a judgment. Starting the job earlier is a decision, made by the decision owner. Who makes the change, and by when, is the action. A fact states its source, subject, time and scope. A hypothesis states what would refute it. An inference states its reasoning and alternative explanations. A judgment states its criterion, confidence and limits. A decision states who made it, the trade-off, and what would trigger a revisit. An action states its owner, deadline and completion condition.

Understanding a problem takes the smallest model that will do, not a longer list. First, model the relationships: one meaning per concept within a discussion, then the causes, dependencies, constraints and feedback loops. Don't force interacting parts into separate boxes, and don't count one effect twice. Next, pin down the observations: give important facts their source and time, and say how each unknown affects the decision. Then test the hypotheses. Look for counterexamples, check the baseline, and consider not acting. Prefer the smallest experiment that separates the plausible explanations. Take last night's delay. Checking the vendor's publication time separates two explanations: did the vendor publish late, or did our job start late? Before trusting a conclusion, check the reasoning for traps such as a correlation presented as a cause, one case taken for the whole, or a necessary condition treated as sufficient. Watch, too, for one outcome blamed on a single cause, selective evidence, or best practice cited without its limits. Finally, bound the conclusion: what the evidence supports, how confident you are, what remains possible, what to verify next, and what observation would change your mind.

When something fails, keep the original symptom and timeline. Find the direct cause, and ask why the existing system did not prevent or detect it in time. Containment, repair and prevention are three layers. Doing only the first two is not a systemic fix. When choosing, compare the feasible options, including the status quo, on the same basis: benefit, cost, risk, reversibility and opportunity cost. Novelty, completeness or popularity does not prove a fit. A recommendation states its premises, the strongest objection, the first step, and what would trigger a revisit. The decision owner decides. “Agreed in principle”, “keep looking” and “keep going” are not decisions. Instead, say: ready for decision, blocked, or deferred. When new facts overturn a conclusion, correct it, rather than defending sunk costs.

Data earns trust only after it is shown fit for a specific use. A file that opens, a chart that looks right, a promising model signal: none of them alone establishes meaning, quality, permission, or reproducibility. So ask six questions. Where did it come from? When did it become available? What does it mean? What changed it? How good is it? How may it be used? Data with unknown meaning, quality or historical availability may be explored within an authorized scope, but stays out of sharing, production and decisions until it is shown fit. Reports, catalogs, caches and Agent summaries are derived, so none may quietly be treated as the source of truth.

One price has at least five moments. A trade sets it in the market. The vendor publishes it. We collect it, then write it into the receiving system. Finally, it becomes available to the people or systems that need it. That last moment is availability time. It is not when you look the value up today: available now does not mean available then. Keep the history of revisions, backfills and derivations, so you can explain each value and when it became available.

Here is an illustrative case. A vendor corrects a stretch of historical prices, and a researcher wants to backtest a strategy, simulating decisions made before the correction. The test needs the values available at each decision time, not today's corrected series. Without knowing when each value first became available, the backtest cannot claim it used only what was available then. Find the earlier snapshots and correction history. Authorized exploration may continue, if it states that the timing is unproven. If a value became available only after the decision, the decision could never have used it. Replace or exclude it, and rerun the test. Bias may hide elsewhere: in sample selection, changed tickers or issuers, the trading calendar, or keeping only today's survivors. Say how missing, late or conflicting data changes the conclusion. Today's accurate feed cannot prove what was available then. Within its delegation, an Agent may locate snapshots, compare revisions and rerun the test, but cannot grant admission: permission for one specific use under stated conditions. The task lead takes the evidence and the open risks to the decision owner and the acceptor, who confirms the agreed result.

Before data enters a lasting work system, eight conditions must all hold. Its meaning is explainable, its source traceable, its time identifiable. Its process is reproducible, its quality verifiable, its operation observable. Its owner is identifiable, and its use is bounded. If any condition is unmet, label the data exploratory, temporary, limited-use, or awaiting verification. A limited use needs its own evidence and permission, but does not make the data a fully qualified asset. From a signal to controlled use, ask the questions that apply to the intended use. Is evaluation worthwhile? What is the data? Can the key findings be reproduced? Can it run reliably? May it serve this use? And does feedback support continuing? A signal is not yet production-ready. Deployed does not mean admitted, let alone for another use. Admission permits a specified use, like that backtest, before it begins. Adoption is that use, actually observed under the stated conditions.

Now an illustrative change. A shared dataset changes its issuer identifier. Local tests pass, but a downstream factor calculation still joins on the old one, and may match the wrong entities or lose some matches. Several decisions are now open. Authorized domain owners settle entities, relationships, rules and metric meanings. The data-model owner settles the grain, the IDs and keys, constraints, and the time and version rules. A convenient schema cannot resolve a conflict of meaning. Development checks the joins and technical owners check compatibility. The product owner identifies dependent consumers and their migration paths, the production owner plans the release and recovery, and governance confirms permission and the exit conditions. These are responsibilities, not seats. One accountable task lead coordinates them without taking over anyone's authority. Before retiring the old identifier, show that every affected consumer has migrated, or that the decision owner has ended its use. A deprecation notice alone does not prove migration.

Engineering owners carry a result from requirements and design through implementation, verification, release, operation and improvement, as the work requires. Development and verification belong to one responsibility. Quality is not passed to the next stage. Before handing a result over, its owner states the criteria tested; the actual inputs, version, environment and results, with what was not covered; and each failure, its handling, and who owns the remaining risk. Changes to production, shared assets or critical management chains, such as risk control or regulatory reporting, need more. They need a defined subject, impact, task lead and authority; inputs and outputs that can be rerun exactly; tests, acceptance and operational observation; and a release window with a rollback or degradation path. They also need security, permission and sensitive-data checks, and conditions to escalate, hand control to a person, or stop. Evidence must come from the current version and environment. The decision owner must approve high-risk use admission, permission changes, production releases, deletion or overwrite, and irreversible actions. An Agent may implement or help verify them within its delegation, but cannot approve them.

Trusted data still needs delivery that can be verified. Before acting, make the commitment, owner and completion condition visible. Delivery is a chain of trust. Name the decision owner and subject; agree on the deliverable and its scope; act only within it; produce current evidence; claim only what it supports; then accept and learn. Lower risk means less process, but the chain cannot skip a link. Before changing anything, check the actual target, version, environment, current state, concurrent work and recovery path. If scope or risk changes materially, return to the decision owner, and record the change and decision in the existing work record.

Report the state, not the effort. With the problem, scope or completion condition still missing, work is unframed. With a route and an owner but no action yet, it is planned. Under way but not yet verified, it is executing. The deliverable exists but checks have not passed: awaiting verification. Checks passed for a stated subject, version, environment and limit: verified. Confirmed by an authorized acceptor: accepted. Published at the agreed destination does not mean in use. To claim use, say what was actually observed. Work that's executing is not “almost done”. When blocked, name the gap, its impact and the escalation.

Say complete only when every condition holds. The deliverable is at the agreed location. Every completion criterion is met. Current verification matching the claim has passed. Risks, limits, uncovered cases and follow-up owners are recorded. The agreed state, such as published, is reached. And where human acceptance is required, the acceptor has accepted it. Every completion claim must answer the same questions. What is claimed, about which subject and version? Verified when, by whom, and how? Covering what, and leaving what unproved? A passing test proves neither overall correctness nor acceptance. And an Agent reporting completion does not make the work complete. When evidence is missing, narrow the claim.

The work runs as a loop, not a fixed sequence of stages: frame, analyze, decide, execute, verify, learn. When evidence or risk changes, review the judgment it affects; when the subject, outcome or boundaries change, frame the work again. Before calling high-impact or repeated work complete, leave the test, monitor, rule or recovery path it needs with the existing owners. Fixing last night's delay can leave a monitor on the schedule that warns when the agreed time passes without the data. But don't create an evidence directory nobody uses, or a report nobody reads, just to prove effort.

Members delegate search, analysis and drafting to Agents. Agents extend what people can investigate, reason about and do, within a delegated boundary. But an Agent cannot grant organizational authorization or make commitments for people. So delegate with a clear boundary, not a pile of context. A consequential delegation states its purpose and scope, the deliverable, the limits and permissions, how the result will be accepted, and when to stop. For example: compare the vendor's earlier and revised price snapshots without changing anything. Report each value's source and observation time, the differences, and what cannot be verified. Stop if the earlier snapshot is missing or a write to production would be needed. Do not grant admission. Telling an Agent to “just handle it” does not set an authorization boundary. If missing context does not materially affect direction, safety or authority, the Agent proceeds on reasonable stated assumptions. If the target, fact source, responsible person, authority or an irreversible consequence is unclear, it stops and asks.

A piece of work can involve a task lead, an executing member, an Agent, a reviewer and an acceptor. Where policy permits, one person may hold several roles, but a title alone grants no authority to decide or accept. The person who calls an Agent owns its context, permissions, verification and result. Submitting Agent-assisted work means you have understood and checked it, and accept responsibility for its consequences. Delegation transfers the work, not that duty. The acceptor examines the actual work and evidence. If the agreed conditions are unmet, the work goes back to the member for revision within the delegation boundary. If they are met, it is accepted, recorded and handed off. Acceptance never expands permission.

Checking an Agent's output means checking the work, not just its summary. Is the authority to act correct, and is the state current? Are the inputs true and complete? Do the actual changes match the agreed and reported scope? Did verification really run on the current version, in the right environment? Did the decision owner explicitly approve anything high-risk? An Agent first confirms the task, target, current state and responsible person. It moves in reversible, verifiable steps, without incidental changes. It runs checks matching the claim, and reads the complete results before summarizing. Parallel Agents suit only independent questions: by default they do research, review and cross-checking that change nothing, with one person owning the combined result. A majority opinion is not evidence. Even checked output becomes a lasting team fact only once its source and limits are recorded in the authoritative record for that work.

Stop the affected action and escalate in five kinds of situation. One: instructions materially conflict, or the target, fact source or responsible person can't be identified. Two: authority is insufficient, or the action would cross a permission, compliance, security or data boundary, or an irreversible step lacks authorization or recovery. Three: you find unrecognized work, someone else's uncommitted work, or work whose owner is unknown. Four: verification contradicts expectation, or evidence has expired. Five: continuing would hide a failure, corrupt a source of truth, worsen the harm, or present a guess as fact. Independent authorized work may continue. A completion report names the work, the delivery state reached, the evidence, its limits, and any acceptance still needed. On interruption, preserve the state, uncommitted work, attempts and failures, and where to resume.

Through communication, results become something others can act on. So answer the reader's actual need before recounting the whole process. Name the purpose first: to synchronize, discuss, decide, escalate or review. An important update leads with the conclusion or present state, and what actually changed since last time. Then the decisive facts, impact and risks. Then your recommendation and the decision needed, from whom, and by when. Finally, the next action, its owner and due time, and what will show it is done. When you don't know, say so.

Meetings should produce decisions, not transcripts. Align facts and definitions before comparing options, and locate any disagreement: in facts, inference, values, resources or authority. Challenge propositions, evidence and costs, never personalities or motives. In updates, say things others can check. Instead of “actively progressing”, say: the tests pass, the acceptor still needs to confirm, and we'll conclude by Friday. Instead of “the Agent analyzed it”, say which facts it produced, what you verified, and where the conclusion stops.

Writing aims for fidelity, clarity and elegance, in that order. Fidelity preserves the meaning: actors, conditions, responsibilities and limits stay intact, facts keep their sources, and counterexamples, uncertainty and failure stay visible. Clarity begins with accurate understanding, then puts it plainly and fluently, so the reader can understand and act. Elegance brings taste and aesthetic judgment to rhythm, transitions and form, with precise verbs and concrete nouns, without slogans or pretended depth. If collection ran two hours late, fidelity means writing two hours late, not “roughly on time”. Clarity means leading with the conclusion and the decision needed, then the detail. Clarity has concrete checks: one point per paragraph; important terms defined at first use. Before sending, ask whether a reader outside the work could restate the conclusion, basis, limits and next action. Escalating a risk takes four parts. The risk. Its impact, with the facts and the latest safe time to decide. Containment: what was tried, and its result. And the decision: the options, their costs, your recommendation, and who must decide by when.

The aim of learning is to find the next failure earlier, judge it more easily, and need less manual rescue, not to add files. Before a rule or Agent workflow becomes department practice, show an observed problem, a material risk with evidence, or an obligation that applies. Explain why the existing mechanism falls short, and what the new practice would cost to understand and maintain. Require a bounded trial, evidence of net benefit, a named owner, and stated triggers for revision or retirement. Necessary safeguards must not wait for an incident, or for a new practice to be approved. Meet the obligation now, within existing authority; a trial decides only how to meet it, not whether. Retire a rule that has nothing left to govern, has no user, duplicates a source of truth, or costs more than it returns.

Many system defects begin as small signals: definitions drifting, questions recurring, people stepping in to rescue, evidence expiring, ownership blurring, intermittent failures, slight delays. One anomaly is not a trend. Check the pattern, impact and direction of the signals first. When someone asks every week how the same metric is calculated, that is a signal. Watch for these conditions. A problem recurs, or affects more than one person, project or work cycle. An important judgment rests on one or a few people's tacit knowledge. Forgetting something could cause material loss. Or the work is something Agents will repeat. When any of these brings material risk, leave reusable prevention behind, even before anything fails. It can be a test, a monitor, a checklist, a decision record, or clearer ownership and handoffs. Improve what exists before creating something new, and choose the lightest form that works.

Capability grows through real work. Confirm the boundary and success criteria at the start, examine the reasoning at important decisions, and after delivery, agree on one observable improvement for the next task. Coaching tests a member's reasoning without making the judgment for them. Feedback names a proposition, evidence, behavior and consequence. A label like “weak logic” gives no direction. Fluency, effort and Agent efficiency never make up for risks like these: fabricated, selectively hidden or unverified facts presented as established; completion claimed without current verification; Agent output standing in for fact, authorization or responsibility. If work is scored, the score expresses delivery risk. It must never label a person. Ask what decision a metric supports and how it could be gamed, and investigate anomalies through cases and mechanisms. Escalate high-risk signals at once.

Managers clarify direction, priorities, decision boundaries and resources, resolve cross-domain conflicts and stalled decisions in time, and protect people who honestly expose problems. When goals conflict, priorities drift, resources run short or decisions stall, repair the management system before blaming a member. Managers show how they judge through real work, and never use these guidelines for fault-finding, ceremonial review or micromanagement. Within the stated boundaries, the responsible person closest to the facts chooses the method, tools and path. It is an emergency when a data error, production failure, permission or compliance breach threatens data, production, downstream use or compliance. Whoever finds it contains it within their authority and tells the data or production owner. The owner of the affected work sees the response through, escalates to the decision owner for any further permission, and preserves the inputs and versions needed to establish the impact and verify a correction. Urgency does not make an uncertain result reliable, or grant authority. If recording would delay the response, contain first, then complete the record: who decided what, on which facts and authority, how long the measure lasts, who takes over, and when to roll back. Then verify the correction and review the cause. Treat a recurring exception as a mechanism problem.

Back to the price dataset we began with. Its next user needs clear meaning, the facts available at the time, evidence that matches each claim, and an admitted, bounded use. With these, a finished dataset becomes something others can safely rely on. Publishing these guidelines, adopting them, and improving the work are three different results, and only real work can show the last two. Was the problem solved? Was the judgment tested, its limits clear? And next time, can the system recognize it earlier, or handle it better? If the answer to all three is yes, the work has value.
